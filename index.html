Letters, lines,
and the space between.

An interactive field guide to digital type: the parts of a letter, the lines a font is drawn to, how glyphs are placed and moved in a line of text, how Chinese, Japanese and Korean type sits in its square, and how one family descends through five centuries of revivals.

I

Letter

Typographers name the parts of letters the way anatomists name bones. The names are old, and they persist because the parts do: a stem, a bowl, a serif mean the same thing in a punchcutter’s shop and in a font editor. Hover a label to isolate the part; click to pin it.

II

Font

A font file is a set of drawings and a set of numbers. Pick one of the open-source families below, or drop in a font of your own: it stays in your browser. Click any glyph to see its outline, its points and the box it is set in.

A font never says how tall its letters are. It says where its lines are: an ascent and descent reserved around a baseline, and a cap height and x-height the designer drew to. Layout engines stack lines of text using those numbers, not the ink. Pick a line to see where it falls, and how the first baseline and the leading follow from it.

III

Text

Laid-out text is a tree: a frame holds lines, a line holds segments, a segment holds glyphs. Every glyph is finally drawn with one matrix, the product of every level above it: M = F · L · W · P · S. An animation is one more term, A, inserted at the level it moves. Store it as a delta keyed by index at that level rather than as a final matrix: when the text reflows, the layout terms change, and a stored M would be stale.

IV

CJK 方块字

Chinese, Japanese and Korean type is designed inside a square. Each character occupies one em, and text is a grid of these squares, set horizontally or vertically. Punctuation shares the square too, and where its ink sits inside it differs between Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese, even for the same code point.

Because every character is the same width, a line can break almost anywhere, but not quite. Line-breaking rules (禁则 in Chinese, 禁則処理 in Japanese) keep closing punctuation off the start of a line and opening punctuation off its end. The fixes are to hang the punctuation in the margin, to squeeze punctuation so one more character fits, or to push a character down to the next line and spread the remaining ones.

The same text can run top to bottom, right to left. In vertical setting, punctuation takes vertical forms, Latin words turn on their side, and short numbers can stand upright in a single square. Where CJK meets Latin, a quarter-em gap keeps the two scripts from colliding.

VI

Lineage

Draft. A typeface is rarely one design. “Garamond” names a family of interpretations, and some of them are not based on Garamond at all.

  1. 1540sClaude Garamont cuts the romans in Paris that printers would copy for two centuries.
  2. 1615Jean Jannon, in Sedan, cuts types in the same tradition. They are later misattributed to Garamond.
  3. 1917American Type Founders’ Garamond, drawn by Morris Fuller Benton and T. M. Cleland from Jannon’s types.
  4. 1926Beatrice Warde, writing as Paul Beaujon, shows that the “Garamond” revivals are Jannon’s work.
  5. 1967Jan Tschichold’s Sabon, a Garamond design built to look the same on hand, Monotype and Linotype machines.
  6. 1989Adobe Garamond by Robert Slimbach, drawn from Garamond’s romans and Granjon’s italics.
  7. 2011EB Garamond by Georg Duffner, an open-source revival and the serif on this page.
